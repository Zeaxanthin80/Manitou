What a SKU is

Stock Keeping Unit. The identifier for one distinct sellable thing — specifically, the level at which you count stock.

If a shirt comes in three sizes and two colours, that is one product and six SKUs, because six separate piles exist on the shelf and each can run out independently. "How many Classic Cotton Tees do I have?" is not an answerable question in a warehouse. "How many TEE-CLS-M-BLK?" is.

Manitou already has a unique identifier for a variant — product_variants.id, a bigint primary key. So why carry a second one? Because they have different audiences:

IdentifierAudienceLives
id
technical key		 The database, and code that joins on it Inside the system only. An implementation detail nothing outside should depend on
sku
business key		 People, and other companies' systems On labels, paper, screens, phone calls, spreadsheets, and other firms' databases

A picker reading TEE-CLS-M-BLK knows to fetch a classic tee, medium, black, without looking anything up. Reading 76 tells them nothing, is easy to transpose, and means something different in every other system it touches.

A SKU is internal. There is no registry, no authority and no format anyone can enforce on you. Your TEE-CLS-M-BLK and another shop's are unrelated strings that happen to match. Uniqueness is a promise you make to yourself, inside your own database — which is exactly what uq_product_variants_sku enforces in Manitou.
01

Where it actually appears

The SKU earns its existence outside the database. Each of these is a place where a bigint primary key would be useless or dangerous.

Shelf and bin labels

Printed and stuck to the location where the stock physically lives. Usually SKU + short description + a barcode. The label is how a human confirms they are standing in front of the right box. This is the one case where a structured SKU pays off most: shelves are usually arranged so related SKUs sit together, and a code that sorts sensibly makes the rack walkable.

Pick lists

When an order comes in, someone gets a list of what to collect. It is the SKU, the quantity, and the location — not the product name, because "Miami Tee" and "Miami T-Shirt" are indistinguishable at a glance and the whole job is not mixing them up. The pick list is usually the first place a bad SKU causes real money to be lost, because the wrong item goes in the box and comes back as a return.

Barcodes

A barcode is a way of writing a string so a machine can read it reliably. Which string? Whichever one you chose. On your own internal labels, that is the SKU. Section 2 covers how this actually works.

Supplier purchase orders

When you reorder, you send the supplier a document listing what you want. Here is the subtlety: your SKU means nothing to your supplier. They have their own. A purchase order usually carries both — their part number so they can fulfil it, and yours so you can match the delivery back to your own stock when it arrives. Mature systems store the supplier's code alongside your SKU for exactly this translation.

Stocktake sheets

Counting what is physically present and comparing it to what the system claims. Someone walks the shelves with a scanner or a clipboard and records a number against each SKU. Hand-written stocktakes are why ambiguous characters matter: a 0 recorded as O, or 1 as I, produces a count against a SKU that does not exist, and the real one silently stays wrong.

Courier manifests and packing slips

The paperwork that travels with the parcel. The packing slip lists SKUs and quantities so the customer — and anyone processing a return — can verify the contents against what was ordered. For customs, some carriers also want a description and commodity code, which is separate from the SKU and not something a SKU can substitute for.

The spreadsheet the vendor actually uses

This is the one nobody designs for and everybody has. Reorder planning, margin calculations, supplier price comparisons, "what sold last month" — all of it happens in a spreadsheet, keyed by SKU, because the SKU is the only identifier that survives being exported from one system and pasted into another. Section 4 covers the ways a spreadsheet will quietly corrupt it.

02

Barcodes and POS scanners

The mental model people usually start with is wrong in a way that matters, so it is worth being precise.

A barcode scanner does not know what a SKU is. It does not know what a product is, or a price. It is an optical device that decodes a printed pattern into a string of characters and hands that string to whatever software is listening. All the meaning is added afterwards, by a database lookup.

What physically happens at a till

1.  Scanner reads the bars, decodes them to characters   "0712345678912"
2.  Scanner emits that text, usually as keystrokes,
    exactly as though someone had typed it very fast,
    followed by an Enter                                  (keyboard-wedge mode)
3.  The POS software has a search box focused. The text
    lands in it and the Enter submits.
4.  POS queries ITS OWN database:  WHERE gtin = '0712345678912'
5.  Row found -> name, price, tax rate, stock decrement.
    Row not found -> "unknown item".

Step 5 is where everything you care about lives, and it is ordinary database work — the same shape as SELECT ... WHERE sku = $1. The scanner replaced typing, nothing more. Many scanners can also run over USB serial or Bluetooth rather than pretending to be a keyboard, but the principle is unchanged: symbol in, string out, lookup follows.

Two different strings can be in that barcode

Retail barcodeInternal barcode
Encodes A GTIN — a globally unique number (Section 5) Your SKU, or your own internal id
Printed by The manufacturer, on the product or its packaging You, on a label you stick on the shelf or the item
Symbology UPC-A or EAN-13 — fixed length, digits only Code 128 or Code 39 — variable length, letters allowed
Understood by Any shop in the world that stocks the item Only you
This is why SKUs are conventionally uppercase. Not aesthetics — encodability. Code 39, the simplest and most widely supported alphanumeric symbology, can only represent A–Z, 0–9, space and - . $ / + %. There are no lowercase letters in it at all. A SKU of tee-cls-m-blk cannot be printed as a Code 39 barcode; TEE-CLS-M-BLK can. Code 128 handles full ASCII and is the better default today, but the convention was set by the older constraint and there is no reason to fight it. It is also why Manitou normalises SKUs to uppercase on write.

Do you need any of this?

For an online-only store: no. Nothing scans anything. The SKU is doing its work on pick lists and in the vendor's spreadsheet, and a barcode adds nothing until there is a physical counter or a stockroom big enough that walking it with a scanner beats reading labels. It becomes worth it at roughly the point where stocktakes stop fitting in an afternoon.

03

Why TYPE-STYLE-SIZE-COLOUR

There is no standard. There are strong conventions, and they exist because each one solves a problem someone hit the hard way.

Worth being clear about this. GS1 governs GTINs. Nobody governs SKUs. There is no ISO standard, no registry, and no validator that can tell you your SKU scheme is wrong. What follows is accumulated practice, not a specification — but ignoring it has predictable consequences.
TEE-CLS-M-BLK
 │    │   │   │
 └─ type              what kind of thing (TEE / CAP / BAG)
     └─ style         which design (CLS / GRA / GRV / ABS)
         └─ size          S / M / L / XXL / OS
           └─ colour       BLK / WHT / NVY / KHK

Why segments run general → specific

Because sorting alphabetically then groups things the way a human wants them grouped. Every TEE-CLS-* lands together, then splits by size, then by colour. A stocktake sheet sorted by SKU walks the rack in the order the rack is arranged. Put colour first and the same list scatters every product across the sheet.

Why mnemonic and not sequential

TEE-CLS-M-BLK can be decoded by a person with no lookup. 000247 cannot. The moment a SKU needs a lookup to be understood, you have reinvented the primary key and gained nothing for the extra column.

Why fixed, short segments

The eye parses by position. Once you know segment three is the size, you stop reading the rest. Segments of wildly varying length break that, and long SKUs get truncated on labels and in narrow spreadsheet columns.

Things that go wrong

  • Encoding anything that changes. Price, cost, supplier, season, current promotion. When it changes, the SKU is either a lie or must be changed — and a SKU must never change, because every historical order, pick list and spreadsheet already recorded the old one.
  • Reusing a retired SKU. Same problem pointing backwards: last year's sales report silently starts describing a different product. In Manitou this is prevented by accident — every FK into product_variants is NO ACTION, so a variant that has ever sold cannot be deleted and its SKU stays taken forever.
  • Ambiguous characters. O/0, I/1/l, S/5, B/8. Fine on screen, a coin-flip when read aloud across a stockroom or written on a clipboard. Some schemes ban vowels outright, which also conveniently prevents a SKU accidentally spelling something.
  • A SKU that contradicts the item. Worse than an opaque one, because people trust the structure and stop checking. Manitou had TEE-CLS-LG-BLK-3 on a Large/White variant of a product that was not Classic.
  • Uppercase, digits, hyphens. Encodable in Code 39, safe in URLs, safe in filenames, unambiguous in CSV.
04

The spreadsheet will corrupt it

The vendor's spreadsheet is where SKUs go to die, and the failures are silent. This is a real, well-known class of data loss, not a hypothetical.

SKUWhat Excel / Sheets doesWhy
00123 becomes 123 Read as a number; leading zeros are not significant
1-5 becomes 05-Jan Read as a date
TEE-1E5 survives Has letters, so it stays text
1E5 becomes 100000 Read as scientific notation
SEP1 may become 01-Sep Recognised as a month abbreviation

The damage happens on import, before anyone looks. Then the file gets sent back, pasted into a form, and the wrong SKU enters a real system. A prefix of letters — which TYPE-STYLE-SIZE-COLOUR gives you for free — makes every one of these impossible, because a value starting with letters is unambiguously text.

This is the same problem that made the genetics community rename human genes: SEPT1 and MARCH1 kept turning into dates in published data sets. If it can happen to peer-reviewed science, it will happen to a stocktake.
05

GTIN, UPC, EAN and GS1

The layer above SKUs: an identifier that means the same thing in every company in the world, because somebody issues them centrally.

The names, which overlap confusingly

TermWhat it is
GS1 The not-for-profit standards body that issues company prefixes and defines the formats. National member organisations (GS1 US, GS1 UK, …) handle licensing
GTIN Global Trade Item Number — the umbrella term for the number itself, regardless of length
GTIN-12 / UPC-A 12 digits. The barcode on retail packaging in North America
GTIN-13 / EAN-13 13 digits. The international equivalent, used nearly everywhere else
GTIN-8 / EAN-8 8 digits. For packaging too small for a full barcode
GTIN-14 14 digits. Identifies a case or carton rather than a single unit

"UPC" and "EAN" name the barcode symbologies; "GTIN" names the number they carry. In practice people use them interchangeably and usually mean the number.

How the number is built

0 7 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 1 2
└─── company prefix ───┘ └─ item ─┘ └ check

licensed to you by GS1 — identifies your company
you assign, one per distinct product
calculated, not chosen (mod-10)

The check digit is arithmetic over the preceding digits. A scanner recalculates it and rejects the read if it does not match, which is how a smudged or partially-read barcode fails loudly instead of ringing up the wrong item. Note the consequence: you cannot invent a GTIN, because a made-up number will usually fail its own check digit — and if it happens to pass, it belongs to somebody else.

Your prefix length determines how many items you can number. A shorter prefix leaves more digits for items and costs more. That is the whole pricing model: you license a prefix from your national GS1 organisation, typically an initial fee plus an annual renewal, sized by how many products you need to identify. GS1 US also sells single GTINs for sellers who need only a handful.

Avoid cheap resold barcodes. A market exists in reselling numbers from prefixes issued before GS1 required licences to be renewed. They are legal to buy and frequently useless: Amazon and the large retailers now verify a GTIN against GS1's registry and check that the brand matches the prefix holder. A number registered to a company that is not you gets the listing rejected, and you find out after doing the work.

When you actually need one

ChannelGTIN needed?
Your own web storeNo. Nothing outside your system needs to name the item
AmazonYes for most categories, with a brand-registry exemption route for own-brand goods
Google ShoppingRequired if the product has one; listings without are penalised or rejected
Physical retail / wholesaleYes — the buyer's till and warehouse are built around scanning it
eBay, Etsy and similarVaries by category; often optional for handmade or vintage

If you resell someone else's product it already has a GTIN, printed by its manufacturer — you record theirs and assign your own SKU alongside it. If you make and brand your own goods, you assign both: the SKU because you need one internally, the GTIN because your sales channels demand it.

These coexist rather than compete. A single black medium tee can simultaneously be TEE-CLS-M-BLK to you, 0712345678912 to the world, B0XXXXXXXX on Amazon, and row 76 in your database.

07

How Manitou handles it today

  • product_variants.sku is UNIQUE across the whole store, not per product — the point of a SKU is that it means one thing wherever it appears.
  • SKUs are normalised to uppercase and trimmed at the API boundary, so tee-001 and TEE-001 collide and the second is refused with a 409 instead of quietly becoming a second SKU for one shirt.
  • Format is not enforced. Real SKUs carry dots, slashes and underscores, so imposing a shape would invent a rule the vendor never agreed to. Case and whitespace are the only things normalised. Compare products.slug, which is format-checked, because a slug becomes a URL and has exactly one legal shape.
  • Size and colour live in product_variants.attributes (JSONB) as well as being encoded in the SKU. The SKU is for humans; attributes is what filtering and logic read. A variant with a correct SKU and an empty attributes is invisible to every size and colour filter.
  • No GTIN column exists, deliberately. Manitou sells through its own storefront, where nothing outside the system needs to name the item. If the store ever lists on Amazon or Google Shopping, that is a nullable gtin column beside the SKU — an addition, never a replacement.
The rule worth remembering. A SKU answers "which of my things is this?". A GTIN answers "which of the world's things is this?". A primary key answers "which row is this?". Three questions, three identifiers, and confusing them is how a warehouse ends up shipping the wrong shirt.
