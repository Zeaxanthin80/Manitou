How these were built

Not four variations on one idea. The set is deliberately spread across a design space so the choice is genuinely open — and every value in it is constrained by something the plan already commits to.

Step 1 — the constraints came first

Section 4.3 4–6 colours named by role, never by appearance. A display face and a body face. So each direction is exactly two typefaces and a six-slot palette — not a free-for-all.
Quality floor Body text must hit WCAG AA (4.5:1). Every ratio below is computed against that palette's own background, and anything that fails is labelled decorative-only.
Section 1.1 The repo must clone to other store types by changing config and content, not architecture. That makes transplantability a functional requirement, which is why it gets a whole column.
Practicality All seven typefaces are on Google Fonts — free, self-hostable for Phase 6, and available in the weights each direction needs.

Step 2 — spread across three axes

If they all clustered in one corner you'd be picking a shade, not a direction. B, E and F are the expressive end; A and D the restrained ones. Only C commits to a dark canvas, which turns out to be the single biggest factor in how it transplants.

E and F were added later, on request, and slot into the warm/expressive quadrant alongside B. They are not variations of it: B is faded and rustic, E is flat and graphic, F is saturated and celebratory. Where B looks handmade, E looks printed — which turns out to matter for a store that sells printed things.

Step 3 — the type pairing rule

Each pairing needs a real contrast of role: the display face carries personality at large sizes, the body face disappears at 15px. Pairing two similar sans faces produces a muddle where neither job is done well. So each direction pairs one characterful face with one neutral workhorse — and in every case the body face is chosen for legibility in a dense product grid, not for looks.

Why the photo placeholders are light grey

Every card below uses the same neutral placeholder, standing in for a white-background studio product shot — the most common kind of e-commerce photography, and what a small vendor is most likely to have.

Watch how it sits on each background. On the light directions it blends in. On the dark one it floats as a bright rectangle. That is not a rendering artefact — it is the actual problem, and it is why photography style constrains palette far more than most people expect.

How it transplants

T-shirt

Native. Monochrome chrome plus one signal colour is the default language of apparel retail. The garment supplies all the colour the page needs.

Candle

Works unchanged. Same logic — a neutral frame flatters a warm product shot. Reads premium rather than cosy, which suits a design-led candle brand more than a rustic one.

Grocery

Works, and is the best of the four here. Dense grids need high contrast and clear hierarchy, which this has. Archivo Black handles price stacks and unit pricing legibly at small sizes, and the accent gives you a real "on offer" colour that nothing else is competing for.

Correction

This panel originally listed the accent as #E11D48 at 4.8:1. That ratio was estimated, not computed. Measured, it is 4.497:1 on the background — failing AA for small text by 0.003, which rounding to 4.50 concealed. The accent is now #DB1B45 (4.73:1), which clears AA in every usage. The lesson generalises: compute contrast, never estimate it, and be wary of a rounded figure sitting exactly on a threshold.

Cost

Its neutrality is the tradeoff: with weak photography there is nothing holding the page up. This direction depends on decent product shots more than the other three.

How it transplants

T-shirt

Good, if the tees have a story. Illustrated or screen-printed designs with provenance sit naturally here. Plain blanks or technical apparel would fight it — the frame promises craft the product has to deliver on.

Candle

Native — this is candle-shop language. Warm cream, terracotta and a serif is close to the default vocabulary for home fragrance, ceramics and coffee. Of the four, this is the one you would choose if candles were the real target.

Grocery

Works for a farm shop, fails for a weekly shop. Fine for a deli or artisan grocer with 80 products. At the multi-thousand-SKU scale Section 10.1 describes, the cream background and serif read boutique, which fights a fast list-building workflow. Fraunces also muddies at 13px in a dense grid — it is a display face doing display work, and grocery cards are mostly small text.

Watch

The ochre accent is 2.4:1 — decorative only. Safe for borders, badges with dark text, or large headings; never for body copy or small labels. That is a discipline the whole team has to keep, and it is the easiest rule in this document to break by accident.

How it transplants

T-shirt

Native, and the strongest of the four for this product. Limited-run tees, drop culture and heavy condensed caps belong together. If the store is genuinely streetwear, nothing else here competes.

Candle

Fights the product. Acid green and shouting caps are the opposite of what "cedar, smoke, 40-hour burn" is selling. You would be rebranding, not recolouring.

Grocery

Actively bad — look at the third card. Food photography is overwhelmingly bright and white-background, so every product floats as a lit rectangle on black. You would have to re-shoot or cut out the entire catalogue. Then add that grocery UIs are dense, text-heavy and scanned quickly, and long sessions on a near-black data grid are tiring. This is the direction that would force an architecture change to escape.

Cost

Hardest to walk back. Dark-first is not a token swap: it changes photography, iconography, illustration and email design. Choose it because the store is this, not because it looks striking on a comparison page.

How it transplants

T-shirt

Good for quiet basics, wrong for loud graphics. Premium blanks, muted colourways, minimal branding. A bold printed range would sit awkwardly inside this much restraint.

Candle

Native. Home fragrance, skincare and ceramics are where this language lives. Slightly cooler and more clinical than B — modern apothecary rather than farmhouse.

Grocery

Workable but compromised. The palette is fine; the spacing philosophy is the problem. This direction earns its calm from generous whitespace, and grocery needs density — many items visible at once. Tighten the spacing to fit and you have kept the colours while losing the character. Playfair also has high stroke contrast, so it thins out at small sizes.

Watch

Clay at 3.2:1 is decorative only, and sage at 5.1:1 clears AA but not by much. Low-contrast palettes drift below the line easily — one designer lightening a muted tone "just slightly" is all it takes.

How it transplants

T-shirt

The strongest conceptual fit of any direction here. Silkscreen posters and printed tees are the same craft — flat ink, limited colours, bold shapes, small runs. The brand language and the manufacturing process agree with each other, which is rare and worth something.

Candle

Works, leaning market-stall rather than apothecary. The warm limewash flatters amber glass. The slab display reads artisan and a little loud, so it suits a bright, characterful candle line better than a serene luxury one.

Grocery

Genuinely good — second only to Studio. Warm cream handles dense grids without glare, the coral is a natural "oferta" colour with nothing competing for it, and turquoise reads fresh against produce. One adjustment: Alfa Slab One is too dense below ~16px, so product names use Karla bold and the slab is reserved for prices and headings. That split is visible in the cards above.

Watch

Specificity cuts both ways. This borrows from a living culture, and it reads as identity when there is a real connection — the owner, the products, the story — and as costume when there is not. That is a judgement only you can make, and it matters more than any contrast ratio on this page.

How it transplants

T-shirt

Very good for bright graphic tees — festival merch, bold prints, colour-forward ranges. Would fight a muted or monochrome collection, where the interface would be shouting louder than the product.

Candle

The weakest fit in this row. Magenta and gold at full saturation pull against everything "slow-burning, scented, calm" is selling. Viable only for a deliberately playful candle brand; wrong for a serene one.

Grocery

Surprisingly strong — arguably its best transplant. Bright saturated palettes are the native language of markets and produce aisles; magenta and gold read fresh and good-value rather than premium. Rounded 12px corners and a friendly body face suit a fast, tap-heavy shopping flow, and warm white handles dense grids fine.

Watch

Two things. Gold is 2.0:1 — never body text; it works as a badge fill with dark text, as shown. And a saturated palette needs restraint: if every element competes, nothing reads. Pick one loud thing per screen.

Type honesty

Rubik 800 and Nunito Sans are both sans faces, so their contrast comes from weight and width rather than classification — a weaker separation than the serif/sans pairs in B, D and E. It works because the weight gap is large, but it is the least distinct pairing of the six by my own step-3 rule.

The two things that actually decide transplantability

Reading across the matrix, the same two factors explain almost every "no":

Photography A palette makes assumptions about the pictures. Dark backgrounds require cut-out or dark-shot photography; white-background studio shots float on them. Since a cloned store inherits the code but brings its own photos, a dark-first direction is the least portable thing you can choose.
Density Apparel shows few items large; grocery shows many items small. A direction whose character comes from generous whitespace (D) or a display serif (B, D) loses that character when compressed. Directions built on contrast and a workhorse body face (A) compress without complaint.
What "apparel-specific" actually costs

Section 1.1 promises a cloned store needs config and content changes, not architecture. Direction C keeps that promise for the code and breaks it for the brand: the tokens swap cleanly, but the new store needs a new photography direction, new iconography and new email templates before it looks intentional.

That is not a reason to avoid C — it is a reason to choose it only if this store really is a streetwear store, and to accept that the clone story then applies to the backend rather than the front end.

08

Choosing

Three questions that settle it faster than staring at swatches.

What photos will you actually have? Phone shots on a white sheet? Choose a light direction — A or D. Styled, moody, cut-out imagery? C becomes viable. This constrains the choice more than taste does.
Is the clone story real? If you genuinely intend to spin this up for a second store type, A costs you the least later. If Manitou is only ever this T-shirt store, that consideration disappears and C is fully on the table.
Loud or quiet? Should the interface have a personality (B, C) or should the products supply all of it (A, D)? There is no wrong answer, but mixing the two produces the muddle that most small stores end up with.
Nothing here is locked

These are packages for coherence, not fixed sets. Once a direction is chosen, any part can be swapped — a different accent, a different display face, lighter or darker neutrals. The point of task 1 is to settle the direction; task 2 turns it into design-tokens.json, and that file is where every later change happens.
